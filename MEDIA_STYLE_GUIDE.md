# NotebookLM-Inspired Paper-Craft Image Style Guide

> **Note:** This is a reconstructed production style guide inspired by the paper-craft visual style seen in Google NotebookLM Studio. It is not an official or leaked Google document.

No prompt can make image generation literally error-proof. The best defence is to define the style as a **physical construction system**, not merely say “make it look like paper.” The guide below does that.

## 1. Core visual identity

The image must resemble a **carefully assembled educational illustration made from individually cut pieces of matte paper**.

It should feel:

- tactile but clean;
- handcrafted but professionally art-directed;
- dimensional but not fully three-dimensional;
- playful but not childish;
- simplified but not generic;
- informative rather than decorative;
- warm, approachable and editorial;
- suitable for explaining an abstract concept, process, historical event or technical subject.

The viewer should immediately believe that:

1. every visible object was designed as a flat paper shape;
2. each shape was cut separately;
3. the shapes were stacked in shallow layers;
4. the scene was photographed from the front in a small tabletop studio;
5. the image was then lightly colour-corrected, not digitally painted.

The result must **not** resemble a vector illustration with a paper texture added afterward.

---

## 2. Exact medium definition

Use the following material vocabulary consistently:

> Layered, hand-cut matte cardstock and lightly textured construction paper, assembled as a shallow-relief paper diorama, with visible paper fibres, clean cut edges, restrained layer thickness, gentle edge curling, tiny natural alignment variations and soft contact shadows between overlapping pieces.

### Approved materials

The scene may appear to use:

- 180–300 gsm uncoated cardstock;
- fine-grain construction paper;
- lightly toothy art paper;
- recycled paper with very subtle fibres;
- warm-white drawing paper;
- vellum only for translucent effects;
- corrugated paper only when structurally relevant;
- thin tissue paper only for clouds, smoke or atmospheric elements.

### Prohibited materials

Do not introduce:

- glossy photo paper;
- plastic;
- vinyl;
- polished resin;
- modelling clay;
- felt;
- fabric;
- foam;
- wood;
- metal;
- glass;
- porcelain;
- rubber;
- shiny laminated surfaces;
- synthetic toy-like materials.

Paper must remain the dominant and unmistakable medium.

---

## 3. Paper surface specification

The paper texture must be visible at close inspection but quiet at normal viewing distance.

### Required surface characteristics

- Fine, irregular paper tooth.
- Subtle directional fibres.
- Very low reflectivity.
- Slight tonal variation within each sheet.
- Occasional faint compression or handling marks.
- Softly absorbent colour.
- No perfectly uniform digital fill.
- No heavy grunge.
- No dirty, stained or distressed appearance unless the subject specifically requires ageing.

### Texture intensity

Use approximately:

- **70% clean matte surface**
- **20% fine paper grain**
- **8% subtle fibre irregularity**
- **2% minor handmade imperfections**

The surface must not look like coarse canvas, sandstone, leather or watercolour paper with exaggerated pits.

---

## 4. Construction logic

Every object must obey paper-craft construction rules.

### Layer hierarchy

#### Background layer

- Flat or nearly flat.
- Occupies the rear plane.
- May contain large geometric fields, sky, walls, terrain or abstract contextual shapes.
- Minimal shadow.
- Low detail.

#### Middle layer

- Contains supporting objects, secondary characters, arrows, environmental forms and diagrammatic elements.
- Raised slightly above the background.
- Produces small, soft cast shadows.

#### Foreground layer

- Contains the principal subject, important labels or dominant conceptual object.
- Raised the most.
- Has the clearest edge definition and strongest contact shadows.
- May overlap the middle layer decisively.

### Physical depth

The construction should appear only **5–20 millimetres deep** in real-world terms.

Do not create deep theatrical scenery. It is a shallow paper relief, not a full miniature set.

### Edge behaviour

Edges should be:

- cleanly cut;
- slightly fibrous at macro scale;
- crisp enough to read clearly;
- subtly rounded by paper thickness;
- occasionally imperfect by less than 1–2 millimetres;
- free from torn, fuzzy or melted contours.

### Layer alignment

Use tiny organic variations:

- a leaf may rotate one degree;
- two stacked circles may be fractionally offset;
- an edge may bow almost imperceptibly;
- a small element may sit slightly above the baseline.

These imperfections should communicate hand assembly without making the composition sloppy.

---

## 5. Shape language

All forms should be translated into **simple, cuttable silhouettes**.

### Preferred geometry

- Rounded rectangles.
- Soft circles and ovals.
- Simplified trapezoids.
- Gentle waves.
- Broad arcs.
- Tapered organic leaves.
- Clean irregular polygons.
- Simple stepped forms.
- Thick arrows.
- Layered concentric shapes.
- Modular geometric icons.

### Shape simplification

A complex object should be represented using approximately:

- 3–8 major paper shapes for a simple object;
- 8–20 shapes for a central object;
- 20–35 shapes for a human figure or complex machine.

Avoid hundreds of tiny fragments. The style depends on intelligent reduction.

### Contour character

Contours should be:

- soft and confident;
- recognisable at thumbnail size;
- free from excessive jaggedness;
- neither mechanically perfect nor crudely childlike;
- designed as if they could genuinely be cut with a craft knife.

---

## 6. Composition system

The composition should feel like an editorial explainer frame, not a cinematic fantasy image.

### Primary hierarchy

Each image should contain:

1. one unmistakable focal subject;
2. one supporting contextual system;
3. a small number of explanatory accents;
4. controlled negative space.

The viewer should understand the main idea within two seconds.

### Recommended allocation

- Main subject: 35–55% of the frame.
- Supporting elements: 20–35%.
- Negative space: 20–35%.
- Text, when present: no more than 15–20%.

### Focal placement

Preferred placements:

- central;
- slightly left of centre;
- slightly right of centre;
- balanced around a clear vertical or diagonal axis.

Avoid placing equal visual weight everywhere.

### Overlap

Use deliberate overlaps to demonstrate depth:

- foreground over middle ground;
- arrows passing behind one object and in front of another;
- foliage partially covering structures;
- labels attached with small paper tabs;
- figures overlapping environmental forms.

Do not let unrelated objects collide randomly.

### Negative space

Negative space should feel intentional and calm. It may contain:

- warm off-white paper;
- pale neutral cardstock;
- a very subtle grid;
- faint paper fibres;
- minimal abstract cut-paper shapes.

Never fill every area with decorative fragments.

---

## 7. Camera and perspective

### Default viewpoint

Use:

> Near-frontal orthographic presentation with a very slight elevated tabletop angle, minimal perspective distortion and the visual clarity of a photographed educational paper diorama.

### Camera characteristics

- 50–85 mm equivalent focal length.
- Straight or nearly straight vertical lines.
- No fisheye distortion.
- No extreme wide angle.
- No dramatic forced perspective.
- Moderate depth of field.
- Most important elements in focus.
- Slightly softer rear layers where appropriate.

### Perspective style

Choose one of these:

#### Flat frontal relief

Best for diagrams, processes and concept explanations.

#### Slightly elevated tabletop view

Best for landscapes, journeys and systems.

#### Gentle isometric view

Best for architecture, infrastructure and workflows.

Do not mix multiple incompatible perspective systems in one scene.

---

## 8. Lighting specification

Lighting is essential. Without correct lighting, the image will resemble flat vector art.

### Key light

Use:

- one large diffused light source;
- positioned approximately 35–50 degrees above and to one side;
- soft studio illumination;
- neutral to slightly warm colour temperature;
- broad, gradual shadow transitions.

### Fill light

Use weak ambient fill so that:

- shadow areas retain colour;
- dark shapes do not become black;
- the scene remains friendly and readable;
- paper grain remains visible.

### Shadow design

Shadows must be:

- short;
- soft-edged;
- physically connected to the layer casting them;
- slightly darker near contact points;
- increasingly diffuse with distance;
- directionally consistent across the whole scene.

Typical shadow distance:

- 2–6 pixels for thin adjoining layers;
- 6–16 pixels for raised foreground pieces;
- larger only for clearly elevated objects.

### Prohibited lighting

Do not use:

- hard noon sunlight;
- neon rim lighting;
- glossy specular highlights;
- cinematic blue-orange lighting;
- volumetric god rays;
- heavy bloom;
- dramatic chiaroscuro;
- coloured nightclub lighting;
- HDR contrast;
- black crushed shadows.

---

## 9. Colour system

The style works best with a **restricted, harmonious editorial palette**.

### Palette size

Use:

- 1 dominant background neutral;
- 2–4 principal colours;
- 1 dark anchoring colour;
- 1 optional accent colour.

Total major colours: approximately **5–7**.

### Colour qualities

Colours should be:

- matte;
- slightly muted;
- warm or gently balanced;
- clear enough for educational distinction;
- less saturated than children’s craft paper;
- richer than washed-out pastel illustration.

### Recommended base palette

A reliable NotebookLM-inspired palette:

- Warm paper white: `#F5F1E7`
- Soft cream: `#E9DFC9`
- Muted coral: `#D87968`
- Burnt orange: `#C46F3B`
- Mustard: `#D6A444`
- Sage: `#849C7A`
- Dusty teal: `#4F8485`
- Slate blue: `#587083`
- Deep navy-charcoal: `#26343A`

Use a subset, not all colours simultaneously.

### Colour distribution

A useful ratio:

- 55–70% warm neutral;
- 20–30% principal colours;
- 5–12% dark contrast;
- 2–5% bright accent.

### Colour separation

Adjacent layers should differ enough in:

- hue;
- value;
- saturation;
- or edge shadow.

Avoid placing two nearly identical shades directly on top of one another unless a shadow clearly separates them.

### Prohibited colour treatment

Avoid:

- fluorescent colours;
- cyberpunk gradients;
- rainbow palettes;
- excessive primary colours;
- glossy candy colours;
- deep photographic blacks;
- metallic gold;
- chrome;
- heavy sepia;
- oversaturated red and blue combinations.

---

## 10. Human figures

People should look like sophisticated editorial paper characters.

### Anatomical treatment

- Simplified but proportionally coherent.
- Clearly separated head, hair, torso, arms and legs.
- Hands represented cleanly with simple silhouettes.
- Five fingers are not necessary at small scale.
- No grotesque joints.
- No duplicated limbs.
- No melted anatomy.
- No doll-like plastic skin.

### Faces

For small figures:

- two minimal eye marks;
- one restrained mouth shape;
- optional simple nose;
- no photorealistic pores;
- no detailed eyelashes;
- no glossy eyes.

For close figures:

- face assembled from layered paper planes;
- features represented through simple paper shapes or restrained printed marks;
- expression readable but understated;
- cheeks and facial volume created by overlapping shapes, not digital airbrushing.

### Clothing

Clothing should be:

- built from separate coloured paper panels;
- simplified into broad silhouettes;
- minimally folded;
- free from complex textile patterns;
- textured like paper, not cloth.

### Diversity and representation

When people are requested, explicitly define:

- age range;
- skin tone;
- hairstyle;
- clothing context;
- body type where relevant;
- role or activity.

Do not let the model default every professional figure to the same generic young man in business clothing.

---

## 11. Animals, plants and natural forms

### Plants

Use layered botanical silhouettes:

- each leaf as one or two paper pieces;
- trunks as broad cut shapes;
- foliage grouped into readable clusters;
- slight edge lifting;
- restrained vein details;
- no photorealistic leaf textures.

### Clouds

Clouds should be:

- overlapping soft-edged paper shapes;
- warm white, pale grey or cream;
- separated by delicate shadows;
- non-glossy;
- neither cotton nor smoke simulations.

### Water

Water should use:

- layered horizontal wave bands;
- overlapping blue-green paper strips;
- subtle directional curves;
- no glass reflections;
- no physically simulated liquid.

### Fire and smoke

Fire:

- nested orange, coral, mustard and cream shapes.

Smoke:

- layered grey, beige or translucent vellum-like forms.

Avoid particle simulation.

---

## 12. Architecture and machinery

Buildings, devices and machines should be reduced to readable modular components.

### Architecture

- Broad wall planes.
- Separate roof shapes.
- Recessed windows represented by inset dark paper.
- Minimal perspective.
- Simplified structural details.
- No photorealistic bricks or concrete.
- No miniature dollhouse clutter.

### Machinery

Represent functional components as:

- circles;
- layered discs;
- bars;
- pipes;
- arrows;
- cut-paper housings;
- simplified interface panels.

Complex technical subjects should feel like a physical explanatory model assembled for a museum exhibit.

---

## 13. Diagrammatic elements

NotebookLM-style visuals often explain ideas rather than merely depict them. Diagram elements must belong to the same physical paper world.

### Arrows

- Thick cut-paper arrows.
- Rounded or clean pointed heads.
- Slight elevation above the background.
- Soft contact shadow.
- Consistent width.
- Maximum three arrow styles per image.

### Lines and connectors

Use:

- thin paper strips;
- printed lines on paper;
- stitched-looking marks only when specifically desired;
- gentle curves or clean orthogonal routes.

Avoid glowing digital connectors.

### Charts

Charts should appear constructed from paper:

- bar charts made from stacked rectangles;
- pie charts made from separate radial wedges;
- timelines made from paper strips and circular nodes;
- process flows made from cards and arrows;
- maps made from simplified layered silhouettes.

### Icons

Icons should be:

- simple;
- monochrome or two-tone;
- physically cuttable;
- consistent in visual weight;
- placed on small paper tiles where useful.

Do not mix 3D icons, emoji, line icons and photorealistic objects.

---

## 14. Typography

Image models remain unreliable with long text. Keep generated text minimal.

### Recommended text usage

Limit each image to:

- one heading of 2–6 words;
- up to three short labels;
- labels of 1–4 words;
- numerals only when essential.

For text-heavy work, generate the artwork without text and typeset afterward.

### Typeface character

Request:

> Clean humanist sans-serif typography, similar in spirit to contemporary Google editorial interfaces, with open counters, moderate stroke width, friendly geometry and excellent legibility.

Do not request a proprietary Google font unless you are typesetting externally.

### Text treatment

Text may appear:

- printed directly onto a flat paper card;
- cut from dark paper as individual letterforms;
- placed on a raised label strip;
- inside a rounded rectangular paper panel.

### Typography rules

- Sentence case preferred.
- Avoid all caps except very short labels.
- Strong contrast against the underlying paper.
- Generous letter spacing only for tiny uppercase labels.
- No warped baselines.
- No handwritten script unless the whole style is intentionally handmade.
- No 3D extruded letters.
- No chrome or glowing type.

---

## 15. Background treatment

The background should support the scene without competing with it.

### Preferred backgrounds

- warm white paper;
- light cream board;
- pale grey-beige cardstock;
- muted colour field;
- faint geometric paper grid;
- subtle desk-like paper surface;
- layered abstract landscape.

### Optional grid

When using a grid:

- very faint;
- thin;
- low contrast;
- evenly spaced;
- printed or embossed into the paper;
- no graph-paper dominance.

### Background exclusions

Do not use:

- photographic room interiors;
- bokeh;
- galaxies;
- dramatic skies;
- concrete walls;
- glossy gradients;
- digital UI grids;
- seamless plastic studio backgrounds.

---

## 16. Detail level

The image should be detailed through **layering and construction**, not through visual noise.

### High-priority detail

Spend detail on:

- silhouette accuracy;
- paper edge definition;
- overlap logic;
- contact shadows;
- focal subject structure;
- meaningful explanatory elements.

### Low-priority detail

Reduce:

- surface ornament;
- tiny decorative marks;
- microscopic textures;
- unnecessary background props;
- random confetti shapes;
- excessive facial detail.

### Thumbnail test

At 10–15% display size:

- the focal subject must still be recognisable;
- the primary action must remain understandable;
- the main colour groups must remain distinct;
- labels must not become visual clutter.

---

## 17. Handmade imperfection standard

The image should be neither sterile nor rough.

Use controlled imperfections such as:

- 0.5–1.5 degree rotational variation;
- subtle edge waviness;
- tiny misregistration between stacked layers;
- occasional lifted corner;
- slight inconsistency in paper fibre;
- marginally asymmetrical organic shapes;
- extremely faint glue contact lines.

Do not use:

- torn edges everywhere;
- glue blobs;
- fingerprints;
- stains;
- crushed paper;
- random wrinkles;
- badly cut contours;
- kindergarten craft quality.

The governing idea is **expert handmade work**, not amateur crafting.

---

## 18. Rendering specification

Request the following rendering behaviour:

> Photographed practical paper artwork, not digitally painted. High-resolution macro-aware rendering with crisp focal edges, subtle paper fibre, realistic cardstock thickness, consistent shallow-relief construction, soft studio shadows, moderate local contrast and restrained post-processing.

### Sharpness

- Focal paper edges: crisp.
- Surface grain: fine and visible.
- Rear layers: slightly softer if necessary.
- No over-sharpening halos.
- No artificially smooth surfaces.

### Contrast

- Moderate global contrast.
- Slightly stronger local contrast around overlaps.
- Shadows should remain soft.
- Highlights should retain paper colour.

### Grain

Use paper grain, not photographic noise.

### Post-processing

Allow only:

- restrained exposure balancing;
- gentle colour correction;
- slight warmth;
- minimal vignette, preferably none;
- subtle clarity.

Avoid:

- bloom;
- heavy film grain;
- lens flare;
- chromatic aberration;
- excessive sharpening;
- teal-orange grading;
- HDR tonemapping.

---

## 19. Style boundaries

The following distinctions are critical.

### Paper-craft versus paper-cut silhouette art

Paper-craft contains:

- multiple coloured layers;
- visible material depth;
- constructed objects;
- soft shadows.

Silhouette art contains mostly flat black cutouts. Do not substitute silhouette art.

### Paper-craft versus origami

Paper-craft uses cut and stacked shapes. Origami uses folded sheets with angular creases. Avoid prominent origami folds unless explicitly requested.

### Paper-craft versus quilling

Quilling uses curled paper strips. Do not introduce spiral coils or rolled strips unless quilling is requested.

### Paper-craft versus claymation

Paper-craft has flat planar forms and fibrous matte surfaces. Clay has rounded volume, fingerprints and soft sculpted transitions. Avoid clay-like modelling.

### Paper-craft versus vector illustration

Vector work has perfectly uniform fills, zero material thickness and digitally precise curves. The desired style must show real material behaviour.

### Paper-craft versus children’s collage

The desired style uses controlled composition, mature colour, accurate information hierarchy and clean fabrication. Avoid crude school-project aesthetics.

## 20. Master reusable style prompt

Place this block after your subject description.

```text
VISUAL STYLE AND MATERIAL DIRECTION:

Create the entire image as a sophisticated handcrafted paper-craft editorial illustration, inspired by premium educational explainer visuals. The scene must look physically assembled from individually cut pieces of matte, uncoated cardstock and fine-grain construction paper, photographed as a shallow-relief tabletop diorama.

Every object, person, plant, building, symbol, arrow and environmental element must be translated into a small number of clean, cuttable paper shapes. Build the composition from clearly separated background, middle-ground and foreground paper layers. Show restrained but unmistakable cardstock thickness along exposed edges. Include delicate paper tooth, subtle natural fibres, slight tonal variation within each sheet, tiny controlled alignment imperfections and occasional barely lifted corners. Maintain expert craft quality: clean craft-knife edges, deliberate construction and polished editorial art direction.

Use a near-frontal or gently elevated orthographic viewpoint with minimal perspective distortion. The composition must have one immediately recognisable focal subject, a restrained supporting environment and generous calm negative space. Arrange objects with meaningful overlap so that the layered physical construction is obvious. Do not scatter decorative elements randomly.

Illuminate the paper construction with one large, soft, diffused studio light positioned above and slightly to one side. Generate short, soft, directionally consistent contact shadows between overlapping paper layers. Shadows must become darkest directly beneath raised edges and gently diffuse outward. Preserve colour and paper texture inside the shadows. Do not use hard dramatic lighting, glossy highlights or cinematic effects.

Use a limited, harmonious editorial palette consisting of a warm off-white or pale cream background, two to four muted principal colours, one deep charcoal or navy anchoring tone and no more than one restrained accent colour. Colours must appear absorbed into matte paper rather than emitted by a screen. Keep saturation moderate, contrast clear and the overall mood warm, intelligent, approachable and visually calm.

Forms should use rounded rectangles, broad arcs, simplified polygons, layered circles, gentle organic silhouettes and thick paper arrows. Simplify complex subjects intelligently while preserving their identifying structure. Avoid microscopic fragments and ornamental clutter.

The final image must look like a real, professionally fabricated paper illustration photographed in a controlled studio, not a flat vector graphic with an added texture, not a digital painting and not a fully three-dimensional miniature. Prioritise material credibility, silhouette clarity, physical layer logic, readable information hierarchy and consistent paper construction across every element.
```

## 22. More forceful model-obedience block

Some models ignore style instructions buried in prose. Place this near the beginning:

```text

This is not an illustration that merely depicts paper. The depicted scene itself is a real physical construction made only from cut, layered matte paper. Every visible form must obey the geometry, thickness, texture, overlap and shadow behaviour of physical cardstock. Any element that appears painted, plastic, clay-like, photorealistic, glossy, digitally extruded or materially inconsistent is an error.
```

## 25. Quality-control checklist

Reject and regenerate the image when any answer is “no.”

### Material

- Does every major element clearly look like matte paper?
- Are visible edges consistent with cardstock thickness?
- Is the paper grain subtle rather than artificially coarse?
- Are there soft contact shadows between layers?
- Is anything accidentally glossy, plastic or clay-like?

### Construction

- Can each object plausibly be assembled from cut pieces?
- Do overlaps obey a coherent foreground-to-background order?
- Are the shadows attached to the correct edges?
- Are objects lying on or attached to a physical plane?
- Are there unexplained floating pieces?

### Composition

- Is there one obvious focal point?
- Is the image understandable at thumbnail size?
- Is negative space preserved?
- Are secondary elements visibly subordinate?
- Is the image free from random filler?

### Colour

- Is the palette limited to roughly five to seven major colours?
- Are colours moderately muted?
- Is the background neutral enough?
- Is the accent colour used sparingly?
- Do adjoining layers remain distinguishable?

### Lighting

- Is the light direction consistent?
- Are shadows soft and shallow?
- Is there enough fill light to preserve colour?
- Are there any prohibited glossy highlights or dramatic rim lights?

### Style

- Does it resemble a professionally photographed craft construction?
- Does it avoid vector-art sterility?
- Does it avoid children’s collage crudeness?
- Does it avoid deep 3D miniature aesthetics?
- Does it remain educational and editorial?

### Text

- Is all requested text spelled correctly?
- Is there no invented text?
- Are labels brief and readable?
- Is typography flat or physically cut from paper?
- Are long paragraphs absent?

## 26. Regeneration correction prompts

When the first output is wrong, do not repeat the entire original request. Diagnose the failure precisely.

### When it looks like flat vector art

```text
Rebuild the image as an actual photographed physical paper construction. Increase visible cardstock thickness, edge fibres, layer separation and soft contact shadows. Introduce slight natural misalignment between selected pieces. Remove perfectly uniform digital fills. Do not change the subject or composition.
```

### When it looks like clay or plastic

```text
Replace every rounded sculpted surface with flat, cut cardstock planes. Remove plastic gloss, soft clay modelling and volumetric rounded forms. Restore fibrous matte paper texture, crisp cut edges and shallow stacked-paper depth.
```

### When it looks too childish

```text
Refine the image toward premium editorial art direction. Simplify the palette, improve spacing, reduce decorative fragments, sharpen the information hierarchy and use more precise craft-knife silhouettes. Preserve slight handmade character without crude or school-project construction.
```

### When it is too cluttered

```text
Remove approximately 40% of the secondary objects. Preserve only elements necessary to explain the central idea. Increase negative space, enlarge the focal subject and organise supporting elements into two clear groups.
```

### When shadows are wrong

```text
Recalculate all shadows from one large diffused light above and to the left. Shadows must be short, soft and attached directly beneath raised paper edges. Remove contradictory shadows, ambient black patches and floating drop-shadow effects.
```

### When the texture is excessive

```text
Reduce surface distress by 70%. Retain only fine paper tooth and subtle fibres. Remove stains, scratches, heavy grain, torn edges and recycled-paper speckles. The craft should look new, clean and professionally fabricated.
```

### When the paper depth is excessive

```text
Compress the construction into a shallow-relief diorama. Reduce object extrusion and spacing between layers. The entire scene should appear no more than approximately 20 millimetres deep, not like a miniature room or foam-board model.
```

### When typography fails

```text
Remove all generated lettering while preserving blank paper label panels in the same positions. Leave sufficient clean space for typography to be added during post-production.
```

## Essential instruction

The most important sentence is:

> **The scene is not digitally illustrated to resemble paper; it is a real physical scene constructed entirely from cut and layered matte cardstock.**

That distinction usually produces a much more convincing result than repeatedly adding terms such as “paper texture,” “paper art” or “papercut.”
